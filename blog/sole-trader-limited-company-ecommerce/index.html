Sole trader or limited company for your e-commerce business? A straight answer

By James Fitzpatrick, AAT licensed · Accounting Solution · Last reviewed 29 September 2026 · 3 min read

There is no single profit level at which going limited pays. If you take all the profit out as salary and dividends, a company now costs about the same in tax as being a sole trader. Limited companies win when you leave profit in the business, make employer pension contributions, or want limited liability.

The 2026/27 numbers

A sole trader pays income tax (20% to £50,270, 40% above) and Class 4 National Insurance (6% between £12,570 and £50,270, 2% above) on all profit, whether drawn or not. A company pays corporation tax at 19% up to £50,000 of profit and 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief between. You then pay tax again when you take money out, and dividends are taxed at 10.75%, 35.75% or 39.35% after a £500 allowance.

The table assumes one owner-director, no other income and no other employees, a £12,570 salary (which costs the company employer NI at 15% above £5,000) and all remaining profit paid as dividends. It shows what the owner keeps.

Profit before owner's paySole trader keepsLimited company keepsDifference
£40,000£32,868£31,633+£−1,235
£60,000£46,111£46,091+£−20
£100,000£69,311£65,335+£−3,977

At these profit levels the company is level or worse if you take everything out. The gap is not a reason to avoid incorporating, but it is why the old rule of thumb of going limited at £30,000 to £35,000 of profit no longer holds by itself.

When a limited company does help

  • You want to leave profit in the company to fund stock or growth. Profit kept in the company is taxed only at corporation tax, while a sole trader is taxed on all of it.
  • You want the company to make employer pension contributions, which are deductible and avoid personal tax and NI.
  • You want limited liability, so a claim against the business does not reach your personal assets, for products with higher risk or a large stock exposure.
  • You may want to bring in a spouse or partner as a shareholder, which needs proper advice.
  • A company can look more established to suppliers, marketplaces and lenders.

What it costs you

A company must file annual accounts with Companies House and a Corporation Tax return, run payroll for any director salary, and keep fuller records. Accountancy fees are higher, and extracting money is more formal. Incorporation partway through a year needs care, and transferring stock and goodwill has tax consequences. The cleanest time is often the start of a tax year or a new venture.

VAT is separate. You register when taxable turnover passes £90,000 whatever the structure, and your VAT number stays with you personally unless you transfer the business as a going concern. See VAT across Amazon and Shopify and what expenses online sellers can claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

Sole trader or limited company for your e-commerce business?

There is no single profit level at which going limited pays. If you take all the profit out as salary and dividends, a company now costs about the same in tax as being a sole trader. Limited companies win when you leave profit in the business, make employer pension contributions, or want limited liability.

At what profit should an online seller become a limited company?

There is no fixed figure. If you draw all the profit, tax is roughly the same either way. Limited companies pay off when profit is left in the business, pensions are paid by the company, or liability protection matters.

Do I pay less tax through a limited company?

Not automatically. You pay corporation tax on profit and then income tax on dividends, so taking everything out often costs about the same as sole trader tax. Retained profit is where a company helps.

Can I incorporate part way through the tax year?

Yes, but it needs planning. You will have a final sole trader period and a first company period, and transferring stock and other assets can have tax effects, so many sellers pick the start of a tax year.
