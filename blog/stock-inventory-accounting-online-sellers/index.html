How do I account for stock and inventory as an online seller?

By James Fitzpatrick, AAT licensed · Accounting Solution · Last reviewed 29 September 2026 · 3 min read

It depends on your accounting basis. Most sole traders now use the cash basis, where stock is deducted when you pay for it. Limited companies, and sole traders who opt out of cash basis, deduct only the cost of stock sold, valuing unsold stock at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

Two ways to treat stock

Since April 2024, cash basis is the default for sole traders and partnerships made up of individuals. Under it you deduct stock purchases in the year you pay for them and you do not count what is left on the shelf at the year end. You can opt out and use traditional accounting instead. A limited company must always use accruals (traditional accounting).

Under traditional accounting the deduction is cost of goods sold: opening stock, plus purchases in the year, minus closing stock. Closing stock is valued at the lower of what it cost and what you could reasonably sell it for after selling costs (net realisable value).

Worked example. A seller buys £12,000 of stock in the year and sells items that cost £7,500 for £15,000. £4,500 of stock is left at year end. On the cash basis she deducts £12,000, so profit before other costs is £3,000. On traditional accounting she deducts £7,500, so profit before other costs is £7,500.

The total tax over the life of the business ends up similar, because on the cash basis the £4,500 is taxed when it later sells with no matching deduction. The difference is timing: cash basis gives an earlier deduction, but hides how much stock you are actually carrying.

Where sellers go wrong

  • A limited company deducting all purchases in the year, without counting closing stock. That understates profit and can lead to an HMRC enquiry.
  • Not tracking stock held in Amazon FBA or another fulfilment centre. It is still yours until sold, and it belongs on the stock count.
  • Ignoring stock that has become unsellable. Under traditional accounting you can write it down to net realisable value, but you need evidence such as markdowns or disposal.
  • Forgetting that cash basis has rules of its own. If you switch basis, adjustment income can arise in the year you change.

A simple stock sheet showing the SKU, quantity, unit cost and location is enough to start, and cloud tools such as Xero with an inventory app can replace it later. See also how to account for returns, because returned items that go back on the shelf need to return to your stock figure.

Sources: GOV.UK: use cash basis for your business, GOV.UK: expenses if you are self-employed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I account for stock and inventory as an online seller?

It depends on your accounting basis. Most sole traders now use the cash basis, where stock is deducted when you pay for it. Limited companies, and sole traders who opt out of cash basis, deduct only the cost of stock sold, valuing unsold stock at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

Can I deduct all the stock I buy in the year?

On the cash basis, yes, in the year you pay for it. On traditional accounting, including for every limited company, you deduct only the cost of stock you have sold, and unsold closing stock is carried forward.

How do I value stock at the year end?

Under traditional accounting, at the lower of cost and net realisable value, which is what you could sell it for less costs of sale. Damaged, obsolete or heavily discounted items can be valued below cost.

Does stock in Amazon FBA count as my stock?

Yes. Until it is sold to a customer, stock in a fulfilment centre still belongs to you, so include it in your stock count and value it at your cost.
