Is half of a joint bank account always counted for inheritance tax when one owner dies?

By James Fitzpatrick, AAT licensed · Accounting Solution · November 2026 · 4 min read

No. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, HMRC's normal practice is to treat each account holder as owning the part of the account that came from their own money. A joint account the person who died funded alone can therefore be included in full on the inheritance tax return, not just half of it. Scotland is dealt with differently.

What HMRC's manual says

HMRC's Inheritance Tax Manual says that, normally, each joint account holder is treated as beneficially entitled to the part of the account attributable to their own contributions (IHTM15042). Its own example is that if the person who died provided all the money, the whole balance at death goes on the IHT400. Withdrawals are set, as far as possible, against each person's own contributions. The manual also admits that the true legal position is far from clear, so treat the contributions approach as HMRC's working practice rather than settled law.

Alan paid all his savings into an account held jointly with his daughter Beth so she could help with bills. Beth never paid anything in. When Alan dies the account holds £80,000. Under HMRC's normal practice the whole £80,000 is treated as Alan's, even though Beth was a joint holder and the money passes to her by survivorship. Whether any tax is then due depends on the rest of the estate and the reliefs available.

Surviving the owner does not take it out of the estate

Money passing to the surviving holder automatically does not mean it falls outside inheritance tax. HMRC's manual says a person's beneficial share of joint property forms part of their estate for inheritance tax (IHTM15012). The share that counts is decided by who owned what beneficially, which is where the contributions matter.

If the other holder is your husband, wife or civil partner

Where the deceased's share passes to a surviving spouse or civil partner, the exemption means the question of how much was theirs often need not be considered (IHTM15042, IHTM11032). The manual defines the exemption by legal status: lawfully married spouses and civil partners qualify, including those separated, but cohabitants and people divorced before the transfer do not. An unmarried partner or a child on the account is where the contributions question becomes live.

Withdrawals and lifetime gifts

Paying money into a joint account that the depositor can fully withdraw is generally not itself a lifetime transfer (IHTM15043). But if the other holder later takes money out for their own use, that may be a transfer. HMRC's manual says it may enquire into withdrawals of money the deceased provided and another holder took, and that accounts opened shortly before death get particular attention.

Scotland

The rules above are for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. HMRC has separate sections for joint money accounts in Scotland (IHTM15051 and IHTM15054), so if the account or the deceased was Scottish, get advice specific to Scots law.

Evidence the personal representatives should gather

  • The account mandate and terms, any passbook and the opening documents.
  • Statements showing who paid money in, going back as far as the bank holds them.
  • Anything that records why the account was joint: a letter, an email, notes of what was agreed.
  • Records of withdrawals by the other holder and what the money was used for.

The personal representatives complete the IHT400 to show the estate's assets and liabilities (IHTM10021), so it is better to have the answer ready before the form is filled in than to explain it afterwards.

Sources: HMRC manual IHTM15042: joint money accounts · IHTM15043: lifetime gifts into joint names · IHTM15012: joint property and inheritance tax · IHTM11032: spouse and civil partner exemption · IHTM10021: IHT400 introduction. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a joint bank account always split 50:50 for inheritance tax?

No. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland HMRC's normal practice is to treat each holder as owning the part that came from their own money. If the person who died paid in everything, the whole balance can count as theirs.

If the account passes to the surviving holder, is it outside the estate?

No. Passing by survivorship does not take it out of inheritance tax. A person's beneficial share of joint property forms part of their estate.

Does it matter if the joint holder is my spouse or civil partner?

Often it removes the issue, because transfers between lawfully married spouses and civil partners are exempt. Cohabiting partners and people divorced before the transfer do not qualify.

What if the other holder took money out before death?

HMRC may enquire into withdrawals of money the deceased provided and another holder took, since they may be lifetime transfers. Accounts opened shortly before death get particular attention.

Are the rules the same in Scotland?

No. HMRC's manual has separate sections for Scottish joint money accounts, so get advice that applies Scots law.

What evidence should the executors collect?

The account mandate and terms, statements showing who paid money in, any record of why the account was joint, and details of withdrawals by the other holder.
