Can I give my staff a Christmas party and gifts without creating a tax charge?

By James Fitzpatrick, AAT licensed · Accounting Solution · November 2026 · 4 min read

Yes, inside two sets of limits. A party is tax-free for staff if it is an annual event open to employees generally and costs no more than £150 a head including VAT. A gift is tax-free under the trivial benefits rule if it costs no more than £50, is not cash or a cash voucher, is not part of the employee's contract and is not a reward for their work. Go over either limit and the whole benefit is taxable, not just the excess.

The £150 party exemption

HMRC's manual sets out the conditions. The event must be annual, such as a Christmas party or a summer barbecue. A one-off, such as a 25th anniversary party, does not qualify. It must be open to employees generally, or to employees generally at one location if you have several. Cost per head is the total cost including VAT, plus any transport or overnight accommodation you provide so people can attend, divided by everyone who goes, guests included. A virtual event can qualify if the other conditions are met.

It is an exemption, not an allowance. HMRC's manual says the £150 is not an amount to set against a cost that exceeds it. At £151 a head the whole cost per head is taxable, on the employee and on any family or household members who came as guests.

More than one event in the year

If you hold two or more functions, work out the cost per head for each and add them up. You do not need to track who attended which. If the total is over £150, the events that make the best use of the £150 are exempt and the rest are taxable. Say the Christmas party costs £100 a head and the summer barbecue £60. Together that is £160, so both cannot be exempt. Exempting the £100 party leaves the £60 barbecue taxable on whoever attended it, which is the better result for staff.

A party for the directors only

HMRC's own example makes the point. A Christmas party for all staff at an average of £50 a head is exempt. A separate directors-only party at £75 a head is not, because it was not open to staff generally, and the full benefit is taxable on each director who attends.

Gifts and the trivial benefits rule

The statutory trivial benefits exemption has four conditions, and all must be met. The cost of providing the benefit is no more than £50. It is not cash or a cash voucher. The employee does not get it under a contractual obligation, which includes a salary sacrifice arrangement. And it is not given in recognition of particular services the employee performs, or in anticipation of them. The exemption also covers benefits for an employee's family or household and benefits provided by a third party on your behalf, as long as you ultimately bear the cost.

Where one benefit goes to a group, the cost is measured per person as the average, but only where working out the exact cost for each person is impracticable. Cash is out however small, so a cash Christmas bonus is not covered. Gift cards need care, so ask us before buying a batch.

If you are a director of your own company

Where the employer is a close company and the benefit goes to a director or other office holder, or to their family or household, the exemption is capped at £300 of total cost in the tax year. Six £50 gifts reach the cap. Anything beyond it is taxed in the usual way.

Nina runs a shop with 12 staff. Christmas lunch costs £1,400 including VAT and 14 people come, the 12 staff plus two partners. That is £100 a head, under £150, and it is open to everyone, so it is exempt. She also gives each member of staff a £30 box of chocolates. Each is under £50, not cash, and not a reward for performance, so it can be a trivial benefit. A £60 hamper would fail the cost test and the full £60 would be a taxable benefit. This is an illustration only.

What this page does not cover

We have not covered whether the company gets a deduction for the cost, National Insurance, VAT on the event, or how to report an amount that goes over a limit. Those depend on your set-up, so ask before you book.

What to keep

The invoices for the event, the headcount and who attended, who received each gift and what it cost. If you are close to a limit, those records are what show which side of it you are on.

Sources: HMRC manual EIM21690: annual parties and social functions · HMRC manual EIM21691: annual parties, examples · HMRC manual EIM21864: statutory trivial benefits exemption. General guidance on the rules at 19 November 2026; your own facts decide the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a staff Christmas party tax-free?

Yes if it is an annual event open to employees generally, and the cost per head is no more than £150. The cost includes VAT and any transport or accommodation you provide, divided by everyone who attends including guests.

What if the cost per head is over £150?

The whole cost per head becomes taxable, not just the part above £150. HMRC's manual says the £150 is not an allowance to set against a cost that exceeds it.

Does the £150 cover all events in the year?

Yes. The cost per head of each function is added together. If the total is over £150, the events that make the best use of the £150 are exempt and the others are taxable.

Can the directors have their own party?

Not tax-free if it is not open to staff generally. In HMRC's example a directors-only party at £75 a head is not exempt, and the full benefit is taxable on each director who attends.

What is the trivial benefits rule?

A benefit is exempt if it costs no more than £50, is not cash or a cash voucher, is not provided under a contractual obligation and is not a reward for particular services. All four conditions must be met.

Is there a limit for directors of my own company?

Yes. If the employer is a close company, the trivial benefits exemption for a director or other office holder, and their family or household, is capped at £300 of total cost in the tax year.
