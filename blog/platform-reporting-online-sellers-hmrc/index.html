Online marketplaces now report sellers to HMRC. Do I owe tax on what I sell?

By James Fitzpatrick, AAT licensed · Accounting Solution · November 2026 · 4 min read

Being reported does not mean you owe tax. UK digital platforms now collect seller details and send income totals to HMRC each January. Whether you owe tax depends on whether you are trading, not on what the platform sent. Selling your own household things is unlikely to be taxable, but buying or making goods to sell at a profit is likely to count as trading.

What platforms now send to HMRC

GOV.UK says platforms report details of sellers who register to sell goods or services, including renting out property. For an individual that means full name, home address, date of birth and National Insurance number. They also report the total earned on the platform in the calendar year, after the fees, commission or taxes the platform took, broken down by quarter. The information goes to HMRC by the following January.

Who is caught

For sellers of goods, the Low Incomes Tax Reform Group (LITRG) gives the thresholds as 30 or more transactions, or earnings of 2,000 euros (about £1,700) or more, through a platform in the calendar year. It also says that for 2024 platforms could report only sellers who joined that year, and that from the 2025 calendar year they must report all sellers, new and existing. So a seller who was not reported last year may well be reported now.

The statement you should get

Platforms must send you a statement by 31 January for the previous calendar year, usually by email or in your account dashboard, according to LITRG. So the statement for 2026 is due by 31 January 2027. Keep your contact details up to date on each platform, because that is where it goes.

Does a report mean tax is due?

No. GOV.UK says a platform reporting your details does not automatically mean you owe tax. Tax is generally owed if you are trading or making a capital gain. You are unlikely to owe tax on selling personal household items. Buying or making goods to sell at a profit is likely to count as trading, and that is the case that matters for most online sellers.

The £1,000 trading allowance

GOV.UK's guidance on the allowances gives a £1,000 trading allowance each tax year. If your gross trading income is £1,000 or less, you generally do not need to tell HMRC, but you must keep records. If it is over £1,000 you should register for Self Assessment, by 5 October after the end of the tax year. Above the allowance you can either use it instead of deducting expenses, or deduct your actual expenses, whichever is better.

Mia buys vintage clothing at auctions and resells it on a marketplace. Her statement shows £4,200 for calendar year 2026. That is not her tax figure, because the tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April and she must work out the sales that fall within it. Say that gives £3,900 of sales in 2026/27, with £2,100 spent on the stock she sold. Her costs are more than the £1,000 allowance, so claiming actual expenses is better and her profit is £1,800. If she had spent only £600, using the allowance would be the better choice. This is an illustration only.

What to do with the statement

  • Check it against your own records. GOV.UK says to use it to track earnings, and that it does not replace your business records or your tax calculation.
  • Work out your earnings for the tax year, not the calendar year.
  • Look at what the platform deducted. GOV.UK says tax it deducted may be offset against tax due.
  • If you are trading and over the allowance, register for Self Assessment.

When this gets more complicated

If you sell through a limited company or a partnership, the allowance is restricted. GOV.UK says the trading allowance cannot be used for income from a company you or a connected person own or control, or from a partnership. VAT is a separate question with its own thresholds.

Sources: GOV.UK: selling goods or services on a digital platform · LITRG: seller information statements · GOV.UK: tax-free allowances on property and trading income. General guidance on the rules at 16 November 2026; your own facts decide the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does being reported to HMRC mean I owe tax?

No. GOV.UK says a platform reporting your details does not automatically mean you owe tax. Tax is generally owed if you are trading or making a capital gain.

Which sellers of goods are reported?

LITRG gives the thresholds as 30 or more transactions, or earnings of 2,000 euros (about £1,700) or more, through a platform in the calendar year. From the 2025 calendar year platforms report all sellers who meet them, not only new ones.

When will I get a statement from the platform?

By 31 January for the previous calendar year, usually by email or in your account. The statement for 2026 is due by 31 January 2027.

Do I need to register for Self Assessment if I sell things online?

It depends on whether you are trading. Selling personal household items is unlikely to be taxable. If you trade and your gross trading income is over £1,000 in a tax year, you should register by 5 October after the end of that year.

What is the trading allowance?

A £1,000 allowance each tax year against trading income. Below it you generally do not need to tell HMRC. Above it you can use it instead of deducting your expenses, or deduct actual expenses if they are higher.

The statement figure differs from my own records. Which is right?

Your own records are what your tax return is based on. The statement is a check, and it is for the calendar year, while your return is for the tax year from 6 April to 5 April.
